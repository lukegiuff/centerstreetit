A turnaround crew doesn't wing it when a scope changes mid-shift. Someone already knows who makes the call, who gets notified, and what happens next — because the plan was written before the outage, not during it.

Most businesses handle IT the opposite way. An outage, a ransomware hit, a server that won't come back up — and the first conversation about who does what happens while the clock is already running.

That gap is usually one of five specific mistakes. Here they are, in order of how often we see them.

**Mistake #1: Assuming backups are enough**

A backup gets your data back. It doesn't tell you which systems come up first, who's responsible for each step, or how long it should take. Without that plan written down, those decisions get made live, during the outage — which is the most expensive time to make them.

*Fix:* Write down what gets restored, in what order, and who owns each step. One page is enough.

**Mistake #2: Never testing the plan**

A recovery plan nobody has run is a guess dressed up as a plan. Testing is what tells you a backup won't actually restore clean, or that a step is outdated, or that a "quick" restore actually takes six hours.

*Fix:* Run it at least once a year, on your schedule, not the outage's. Anything that goes wrong during the test is the win — that's the whole point of testing before it's real.

**Mistake #3: No one owns the decision**

When something breaks, someone has to decide what gets fixed first and who gets called. If that's not already assigned, the team spends the first twenty minutes figuring out who's in charge instead of fixing anything.

*Fix:* Assign it in advance. Each person should know what they own, who they loop in, and at what point they escalate.

**Mistake #4: Forgetting the people, not just the systems**

Employees need to know what to do. Customers need to know what to expect. Vendors may need direction. If email and phones are part of what's down, normal communication is gone right when you need it most.

*Fix:* Have a communication plan with backup channels, for employees, customers, and vendors — and one person who owns sending the updates.

**Mistake #5: Treating the plan as done once it's written**

People leave. Systems change. Vendors get swapped out. A plan built on last year's contact list and last year's systems can slow a recovery down instead of speeding it up.

*Fix:* Put a recurring review on the calendar, and revisit it any time there's a real change to your team, systems, or vendors.

**Recovery favors the prepared**

A plan that's been tested, with clear ownership and a real way to reach people, is the difference between a bad day and a bad month. None of this requires better technology first — it requires the plan to exist and to have been checked.
