Nobody walks onto a turnaround and works out the lockout on the spot. The permits are pulled, the JSA is signed and everyone knows where the muster point is before the first bolt comes loose. When something goes sideways, the crew doesn't improvise. They already know who makes the call, who gets notified and what happens next. It's the same reason good football teams practice for the fumble, not just the play.

Your office deserves that same planning. A ransomware hit, a dead server or a hijacked email account doesn't wait for a slow week. It shows up the Thursday before payroll, or the morning a plant on the Ship Channel asks for an updated training roster by end of day.

When that happens, the real damage usually doesn't come from the outage itself. It comes from a handful of mistakes that turn a one-day problem into a two-week one. These are the five we see most often.

### Mistake #1: Thinking the backup is the plan

A backup gets your files back. It doesn't tell anyone what to do with them.

If your server went down tomorrow, what comes back first: payroll, the estimating software, your job files, or the folder holding every safety manual and training record you keep current in ISNetworld, Avetta or Veriforce? Who makes that call? How long does each piece take to restore? If nobody has answered those questions ahead of time, they get answered in the middle of the outage, by whoever happens to be in the office, while crews wait on paperwork and the clock runs.

**What to do:** Write a one-page recovery plan that lists what gets restored, in what order and who's responsible for each piece. Put payroll and anything a plant can ask for on short notice near the top.

### Mistake #2: Never testing it

An untested recovery plan is a guess in a binder. It might work. It might fall apart at step two.

Testing is how you find out the backup hasn't made a clean copy since spring, the restore takes nine hours instead of one, or the login to the backup account left with your former office manager. Every one of those is a cheap fix on a Tuesday afternoon and an expensive one during an outage.

**What to do:** Run a real restore at least once a year. Actually bring files back. Don't just check that the backup says "successful." Anything that surprises you goes on the fix list, not the excuse list.

### Mistake #3: Nobody knows who's in charge

The first hour of an outage is full of decisions. Do we shut everything down? Who calls the insurance carrier? Who tells the plant a document might be late? Who's allowed to spend money to get back up and running?

If those answers aren't settled ahead of time, you spend that hour figuring out who gets to make them instead of fixing the problem.

**What to do:** Assign roles now, before anything breaks. Everyone involved should know what they own, who they check with and when to call for outside help. Write it down next to the phone numbers.

### Mistake #4: Forgetting the people

Systems are only half of a recovery. The other half is people. Your office needs to know what to do. Foremen in the field need to know whether timesheets and work orders still go through. Customers need to know whether the crew is still showing up Monday. Vendors may need to hold a delivery.

Now picture handling all of that when company email is the thing that's down.

**What to do:** Build a short communication plan for employees, field crews, customers and vendors. Keep a phone and text list that doesn't live in email. Print it, keep a copy in a truck, and put it somewhere you can reach it cold. Name one person to own the updates so everyone hears the same thing.

### Mistake #5: Writing it once and filing it away

A recovery plan starts going stale the day you finish it. Office staff turn over. You switch accounting software. A new customer adds you to their approved contractor list, and suddenly there's a new set of documents you can't afford to lose.

A plan full of old phone numbers and retired systems doesn't just fail to help. It sends people in the wrong direction at the worst possible moment.

**What to do:** Put a plan review on the calendar at least once a year. Pull it out any time you add or lose key staff, change systems or vendors, or pick up major new work.

### Recovery favors the prepared

The contractors who get back to work fastest after a bad day aren't luckier. They did the thinking ahead of time. A tested plan, clear ownership and a way to reach people when email is dark are the difference between a rough morning and a lost week.

Fixing these five mistakes doesn't take new software. It takes an afternoon of honest answers and someone willing to check the work.
