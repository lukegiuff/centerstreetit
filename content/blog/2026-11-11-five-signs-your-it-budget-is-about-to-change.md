Your technology gives you the same kind of warning. Here are five signs to watch, with a check you can run on each one this week, before next year’s budget gets locked.

#### 1. Your equipment is getting old

Computers, servers, firewalls and switches wear out on a schedule you can predict. The problem is that most businesses can’t say what they own or how old it is, so a replacement that could have been planned a year out turns into an emergency purchase the morning a machine won’t boot.

Age also bunches up. If you outfitted the office in one push five years ago, those machines are aging out together, and your budget will feel all of it in a single year.

Warranty matters as much as age. A server that fails under warranty gets a replacement part on a known timeline. One that fails out of warranty waits for whatever parts you can find, and your crew waits with it.

**Check this:** List every computer, server and network device with its purchase date and warranty end date. Anything five years or older goes on next year’s budget. If a third or more of your computers hit that mark in the same year, that’s a spike you can see coming, and leasing can turn it into a monthly payment instead of one large check.

#### 2. You’re planning to hire

Every new employee needs a computer, licenses, accounts and security setup before they can do any work, and that cost belongs in the budget next to their pay. We broke it down line by line in [What a New Hire Really Costs in IT](https://centerstreetit.com/blog/2026-11-04-what-a-new-hire-really-costs-in-it-five-line-items-to-budget-before-day-one/).

**Check this:** For each position you expect to fill next year, write down what that person needs on day one and what it costs. If that line is blank, the hire isn’t fully budgeted.

#### 3. Your software bill keeps climbing

Software spend creeps. Someone adds a scheduling app. A department moves up a plan tier for one feature. Storage fills and the next tier kicks in. Nobody decides to spend more. It happens one approved charge at a time.

Some of that spend is waste. The most common example we find is licenses for people who don’t work there anymore. An employee leaves, the account gets disabled, and the license keeps billing every month. The second most common is two products doing the same job because nobody cancelled the old one.

**Check this:** Count the Microsoft 365 licenses you pay for and compare that to the number of people on payroll. If the license count is higher, find out who the extras belong to. Then pull twelve months of software charges from your card and bank statements and compare the total to last year. If spending grew faster than headcount, find out why before you carry it into next year.

#### 4. Renewals are coming up

Software subscriptions, support contracts, copier leases, firewall security subscriptions, domain names and warranties all renew on a date. The date that matters is the one before it: the notice deadline. Many agreements renew automatically unless you cancel or renegotiate 30, 60 or 90 days ahead. By the time the renewal invoice arrives, the decision has already been made for you.

Some renewals cost you protection when they lapse. When a firewall’s security subscription expires, the firewall keeps passing traffic and looks normal, but the threat filtering you were paying for stops.

**Check this:** List everything that renews in the next twelve months in three columns: renewal date, notice deadline and current cost. Put the notice deadlines on your calendar, not the renewal dates.

#### 5. Something you rely on is losing support

Software doesn’t have to be broken to cost you money. Once the maker stops supporting it, security fixes stop, and any weakness found after that date stays open for good.

Several of these deadlines have already passed or are close:

* Windows 10 stopped getting free security updates in October 2025.
* Office 2019 lost support in October 2025, and Office 2021 followed on October 13, 2026. techrepublic
* Windows Server 2016 reaches end of support on January 12, 2027.

That last one is two months out. If the server in your closet was bought around 2017, there’s a good chance it’s running it.

The upgrade itself is often the smaller cost. The bigger one is the business application that only runs on the old version and needs its own upgrade to move. And your cyber insurance application asks about unsupported software, so an unsupported machine on your network is exactly where an insurer will look after a claim.

**Check this:** On any computer, open Settings, then System, then About, and read the Windows edition. In Word, open File, then Account, to see the Office version. For a server, ask whoever manages it. Anything on the list above goes on next year’s budget, and so does a call to your software vendor asking whether your line-of-business application supports the current versions.

#### Put it on one page

Each of these checks gives you a few rows: an item, a date and a cost. Put them on one sheet, sorted by date, and you have next year’s IT forecast. For a business with 10 to 50 people it’s usually a short list, and it takes an afternoon to build.
