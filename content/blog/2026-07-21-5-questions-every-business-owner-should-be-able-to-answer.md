You don't have to be an IT person to run your company well, but there are a handful of basic questions about the systems you depend on every day that you should be able to answer without hesitation.

If you can't, that's not a knock on you. It's just a sign there are gaps worth closing.

**1. Who currently has access to your critical systems, and should they still have it?**

Start with the obvious ones: accounting software, your CRM, your email platform. Do you actually know, right now, everyone who can log in to each one?

Access rarely shrinks on its own. A contractor gets added for a project. An employee leaves and nobody remembers to pull their login. Someone needed elevated permissions for one task six months ago and still has them. Piece by piece, more people end up with access than you'd expect, and not all of it still makes sense.

This isn't about trust. It's about exposure. Every login that doesn't need to exist is one more way in for someone who shouldn't be there, whether that's a stolen credential or a former employee who never lost access. The more access has piled up unchecked, the harder it is to contain a problem once one starts.

**2. If a system went down right now, who's actually responsible for fixing it?**

Pick any critical piece of your business — your server, your line-of-business app, your phones. If it stopped working this minute, do you know exactly who owns getting it back up, and how to reach them?

"It depends" or "I'd have to check" is the answer that tells you there's a gap. The more vendors and team members touch a system, the easier it is for ownership to slip through the cracks — everyone assumes someone else has it covered, and nobody actually does.

Downtime is expensive on its own. Not knowing who to call makes it worse. That's a question to have answered before something breaks, not while it's broken.

**3. When did you last actually test your backups?**

Almost every business has backups running. Far fewer have ever confirmed those backups can restore anything.

Setting up a backup job and knowing it can bring your data back are two different things. Backups tend to get configured once, checked off a list, and never looked at again. A backup nobody's tested isn't a safety net — it's an assumption.

Plenty of business owners find this out at the worst possible moment. If you can't remember the last time someone actually tested a restore, you're operating on faith, not verification.

**4. Do you know everywhere your business data actually lives?**

This is the one that catches people off guard, because data doesn't stay put. It spreads.

Every new tool adds another place for it to end up — email threads, shared drives, project management platforms, someone's personal folder. Some of it's in systems you use constantly. Some of it's sitting in an app your team signed up for two years ago and forgot about.

Without a real picture of where your data lives, you can't say who has access to it, whether it's protected, or what you'd do if one of those tools had a problem. That blind spot makes it a lot harder to protect your data consistently, or to answer straight questions from a client, a regulator, or an insurance carrier.

**5. Which vendors have access to your systems or your data — and do you know what they can do with it?**

Vendors accumulate fast. An integration here, an app someone recommended there, a tool that got connected to solve one problem. Nearly all of them come with some level of access baked in.

Vendor access itself isn't the issue. Not knowing the scope of it is. Third-party access is risk coming from outside your walls, and it's usually the thing that gets the least attention on this whole list.

If you had to list every vendor touching your systems today, would you know what each one is actually able to see or do?

## If you're not sure, that's your answer

None of these are trick questions. They're the fundamentals — access, accountability, backups, data, and vendors. When the answers aren't clear, you're looking at a visibility gap, and visibility gaps are cheap right up until they aren't.

Midyear is a good checkpoint for this. Growth changes things quietly — systems evolve, vendors get added, access expands — and the risk is assuming everything behind the scenes kept pace.
