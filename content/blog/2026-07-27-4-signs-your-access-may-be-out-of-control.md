When a business grows, systems access grows right along with it.

You hire new people, bring in contractors, or move someone into a different role, and you hand out the logins and permissions they need to get to work. What doesn't happen automatically is removing those logins and permissions when the project ends, the contractor leaves, or the role changes.

Chances are, nobody's consistently tracking which permissions are still active — which means more people can log into your business today than would if you were setting it up from scratch.

Every unused account is a potential entry point for attackers. Every unnecessary permission increases the risk of insider misuse, intentional or not.

Here are four signs your business isn't managing access the way it should be.

**1. You can't list who has access to your key systems**

If you had to list everyone with access to your core systems right now, could you do it from memory? Is it written down anywhere you could find quickly?

We ask this on every new client engagement, and the answer is almost always the same — nobody knows off the top of their head, and nobody has it handy.

There's rarely one place that holds the full picture. Building that list means checking Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, then your finance software, then your project tools — pulling from different systems owned by different people.

That's not a minor inconvenience. If you ever need to respond to a security incident, that scramble happens under pressure and on the clock.

**2. Access is granted case-by-case, but rarely reviewed**

Most access decisions happen in the moment. Someone needs to get their job done, so they get access — to a confidential folder in Google Drive or SharePoint, say.

What almost never happens is the follow-up: a scheduled review, a clear owner, a defined point where that access gets re-evaluated. Access that was meant to be temporary quietly becomes permanent because nobody came back to pull it.

Over a few years of growth, that compounds into real exposure most owners have zero visibility into.

**3. You're not sure what happens when someone leaves**

When someone leaves, there's usually a checklist — main account disabled, laptop returned, offboarding marked done.

In our experience, it usually isn't actually done.

A former employee's primary login might get closed while their access to a shared drive, a billing platform, or some tool that doesn't get touched daily stays active.

That's a real risk. Former employees with active credentials are one of the most common entry points in small-business security incidents — and it's almost always unintentional on everyone's part.

**4. Different tools are managed in different ways**

Step back and look at how access is handled across your business, and it's rarely centralized. It's scattered across every tool you rely on, each with its own way of managing users, permissions and visibility.

There's no single dashboard showing you everything. Your access picture is spread across systems that don't talk to each other, maintained by different people applying different standards.

The result: outdated permissions go unnoticed because nobody's looking at the full picture — and those are exactly the gaps that get exploited when something goes wrong.

**Start with a clear view of access**

Reviewing and managing access gives you a clear picture of who can reach what, and removes the risk of former employees or lapsed contractors still having a way in. It also speeds up offboarding and makes your response to any security incident a lot cleaner.

If any of this sounds familiar, it's worth taking a closer look at how access is managed across your business.
