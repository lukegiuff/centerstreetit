Hiring should feel like winning. You’re adding people because the work is there. But for most small businesses, the first week of a new hire goes like this: no laptop, no login, a borrowed badge, and someone senior losing half a day getting them set up.

That isn’t a hiring problem. It’s a budgeting problem. Technology is usually the largest cost of bringing on a new employee that nobody priced out ahead of time. Plan these five line items before the offer letter goes out, and a new hire becomes a start date instead of a scramble.

### 1. Computers and Equipment

This is the obvious one, and it’s still the one people underestimate. Depending on the role, a new hire may need a laptop, a desktop, a docking station, monitors, a headset, a phone, or a tablet for the field.

The role sets the price. A dispatcher and a CAD designer don’t need the same machine. Engineering, estimating, and drafting software often needs a dedicated graphics card and more memory, and putting that software on an office-grade laptop guarantees a slow, frustrated employee from week one.

**What to do:** Build a standard equipment package for each role you hire for, with a price attached. When you open a position, the hardware cost is already known. If buying hardware upfront strains cash flow during a growth stretch, leasing spreads that cost into a predictable monthly payment.

### 2. Software and Licenses

Subscriptions feel predictable because they renew on their own. That’s exactly why they get missed. Microsoft 365, your accounting or ERP seat, your scheduling or dispatch platform, PDF editing, e-signature, a password manager, a CAD license. Each one is small. Together they’re a real monthly number, and every new hire multiplies it.

**What to do:** Write down every recurring subscription a person in each role uses, with the monthly or annual cost next to it. That list becomes your per-seat software cost. Then the jump in next month’s bill is something you approved, not something you discovered.

### 3. Accounts, Access, and Security

The costs above waste time and money when you get them wrong. This one creates risk. A shared login, a borrowed keycard, or an account set up in a hurry is exactly how breaches start, and it’s exactly what an auditor or cyber insurance carrier will ask about.

Have these procedures ready before anyone starts:

* **Accounts:**

   Every new hire gets their own logins for email, line-of-business software, and cloud storage, with multi-factor authentication turned on from the first sign-in. No shared shop-floor or front-desk accounts.
* **Physical access:**

   Keycards, fobs, and door codes are issued to the person, not passed down from whoever left last.
* **Security training:**

   New employees learn how to spot a phishing email in week one, before they’re the target of one.

The same checklist works in reverse. When someone leaves, every account, license, and badge gets shut off the same day. Offboarding gaps are one of the most common ways former employees keep access they shouldn’t have.

### 4. Setup and Support Time

Someone has to do all of the above. If you have an IT provider or an internal IT person, that labor may not show up as its own line in your budget. It’s still a cost. Time spent configuring a new hire’s laptop and accounts is time not spent on something else.

One new hire is manageable. Three in the same month can push other projects back or turn into a billable surprise.

**What to do:** Tell your IT provider when the offer is accepted, not the morning the person walks in. Ask how new-user setup is handled under your agreement, what it costs if it’s billable, and how much lead time they need. A setup that’s planned a week out costs less and goes better than one done in a rush.

### 5. The Growth Thresholds Nobody Plans For

One new employee won’t overload your network. Ten might. The problem is that nobody hires ten people at once. Growth is incremental, so the strain sneaks up: the switch with no open ports, the Wi-Fi that drops when the whole office is on a call, the firewall sized for the company you were three years ago, the file server that’s nearly full.

You wouldn’t put 30 people in a 10-desk office. Your technology has a headcount limit too. The difference is that you can’t see it until something breaks.

**What to do:** Know your limits before you hit them. A quick review of your network, storage, and licensing tells you at what headcount you’ll need to upgrade, so the upgrade is a planned budget item, not an emergency.

### Put IT on the Hiring Checklist

Getting ahead of these costs comes down to one change: technology gets a seat at the table when hiring decisions are made. If leadership is approving a new position, they should already know what that person costs to equip, license, secure, and support.

That’s where a managed IT provider earns its keep. Per-user pricing makes the IT cost of each hire a known number, and someone who already knows your environment can tell you what the next five hires will require before you post the job.
